About this translation

The Douay-Rheims Bible was translated from the Latin Vulgate by English Catholic scholars in exile at Douai and Reims (New Testament 1582, Old Testament 1609–1610). The text here is Bishop Richard Challoner’s revision of 1749–1752, the form most English-speaking Catholics read for two centuries. It is in the public domain.

Because it follows the Latin and Greek tradition, a few things look different from modern Bibles:

  • Psalm numbers. From Psalm 10 to Psalm 147 the numbering is usually one lower than in modern Bibles. The psalm most people know as Psalm 23, “The Lord is my shepherd,” is Psalm 22 here.
  • Some book names. 1 and 2 Samuel are called 1 and 2 Kings, 1 and 2 Kings are 3 and 4 Kings, Chronicles is Paralipomenon, and Revelation is the Apocalypse. We use the modern names in the menu and show the Douay name beside them.
  • Older English. “Thee,” “hath” and “saith” are part of its beauty, and of its difficulty.

The readings you hear at Mass in the U.S. come from a modern translation, the New American Bible. You can read today’s on our Bible & Readings page.
