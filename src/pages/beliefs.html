What Catholics Believe

The Catholic Faith, Simply

A short, plain-language overview of what the Catholic Church believes and how Catholics live. For the full, official teaching, every section links to the Catechism of the Catholic Church.

One God in three Persons

Catholics believe in one God who created everything out of love. This one God is three Persons, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. This is the Trinity, the central mystery of the Christian faith. Every human being is made in God’s image and is loved by him.

Jesus Christ

Jesus of Nazareth is the Son of God, fully God and fully human. He was born of the Virgin Mary, taught and healed, died on the cross for the sins of the world, and rose from the dead on the third day. Through his death and Resurrection, he opened the way to eternal life. He founded the Church and sent the Holy Spirit to guide it until he comes again.

The Creed

A creed is a summary of belief. Two are used most:

  • The Nicene Creed, written by the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), is prayed at Sunday Mass. You can read the current English text at the U.S. bishops’ website.
  • The Apostles’ Creed, a shorter and very ancient summary, is prayed at Baptism and in the Rosary. Read it on our Prayers page.

Scripture and Tradition

God reveals himself through Sacred Scripture (the Bible) and Sacred Tradition (the faith handed down from the Apostles). The Church’s teaching office, the Magisterium (the Pope and the bishops in communion with him), serves both by faithfully interpreting them. Explore the Bible.

The seven sacraments

Sacraments are visible signs, instituted by Christ, through which God gives his grace. Catholics celebrate seven, which touch every stage of life.

Sacraments of Initiation

Baptism

Washes away sin and makes a person a child of God and a member of the Church. Received once, with water and the words “I baptize you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.”

Confirmation

Strengthens the baptized with the gifts of the Holy Spirit, through anointing with holy oil called chrism. Received once.

Holy Eucharist

The Body and Blood of Christ, received at Mass. Catholics believe Jesus is truly present, not merely symbolically. It is the “source and summit” of Christian life.

Sacraments of Healing

Penance (Confession)

Through a priest, God forgives sins committed after Baptism. Also called Reconciliation. Everything said is kept in absolute confidence.

Anointing of the Sick

Brings Christ’s strength and peace to those who are seriously ill, elderly, or facing surgery. It can be received more than once.

Sacraments at the Service of Communion

Holy Orders

Ordains men as deacons, priests and bishops to serve the Church. See Church Structure.

Matrimony

A lifelong, faithful covenant between a man and a woman, open to children, that reflects Christ’s love for the Church.

The Mass

The Mass is the central act of Catholic worship. In it, Christ’s one sacrifice on the cross is made present, and the faithful are fed by God’s Word and by the Eucharist. Catholics are asked to attend Mass every Sunday (or Saturday evening) and on holy days of obligation. What to expect at Mass.

Mary and the saints

Catholics honor Mary as the Mother of God, because her Son is God. They do not worship her. Worship belongs to God alone. They ask for her prayers, just as you might ask a friend to pray for you. The Church teaches that Mary was conceived without sin (the Immaculate Conception) and taken body and soul into heaven (the Assumption).

The saints are holy men and women who now share God’s life in heaven. Catholics look to them as examples and friends, and ask for their prayers. Meet some of the saints.

The moral life

Jesus summed up the law in two commandments: love God with all your heart, soul and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matthew 22:37–39). Catholics live this out through:

  • The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20), the foundation of moral life.
  • The Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–12), Jesus’ portrait of a blessed life.
  • The works of mercy: feeding the hungry, visiting the sick, comforting the sorrowful, forgiving offenses and more.
  • Catholic social teaching: the dignity of every human life from conception to natural death, care for the poor, the dignity of work, and care for creation.

When we fall short, God’s mercy is always offered, especially in Confession.

Catholic practices

The precepts of the Church

Five basic duties every Catholic is asked to keep:

  1. Attend Mass on Sundays and holy days of obligation, and rest from unnecessary work.
  2. Go to Confession at least once a year.
  3. Receive Holy Communion at least during the Easter season.
  4. Observe the days of fasting and abstinence.
  5. Help provide for the needs of the Church.

Fasting and abstinence

Catholics aged 18 to 59 fast (one full meal and two smaller ones) on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday. Catholics 14 and older abstain from meat on Ash Wednesday and the Fridays of Lent. Fridays throughout the year are days of penance.

The liturgical year

The Church’s calendar follows the life of Christ: Advent (preparing for Christmas), Christmas, Lent (40 days of prayer, fasting and almsgiving), the Easter Triduum, Easter (50 days, ending at Pentecost), and Ordinary Time. Each season has its own color, which you’ll see on the priest’s vestments.

Everyday devotions

Many Catholics pray the Rosary, spend quiet time before the Blessed Sacrament (Adoration), pray the Liturgy of the Hours, make the Stations of the Cross, and bless themselves with holy water when entering a church.

Life after death

Catholics believe that after death each person is judged by God, who is both just and merciful. Those who die in God’s friendship will live with him forever in heaven, some after a final purification called purgatory. Those who freely and finally reject God choose separation from him, called hell. At the end of time, Christ will return, and the dead will rise.
