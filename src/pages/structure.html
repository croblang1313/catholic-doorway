Church Structure

How the Church Is Organized

The Catholic Church has about 1.4 billion members, yet every Catholic belongs to a local parish, in a local diocese, led by a bishop in communion with the Pope. Here’s how it all fits together.

The current Pope

Pope Leo XIV

Born Robert Francis Prevost in Chicago in 1955, he was elected on May 8, 2025. He is the first Pope from the United States. Before his election he served for many years as a missionary and bishop in Peru, as head of the Augustinian order, and as prefect of the Vatican’s Dicastery for Bishops.

The universal Church

The Pope

The Pope is the Bishop of Rome and the successor of St. Peter, whom Jesus called the “rock” on which he would build his Church (Matthew 16:18). He is the visible source of unity for all Catholics and the chief shepherd of the whole Church. The Pope lives in Vatican City, an independent state in the heart of Rome. Leo XIV is the 267th Pope. Meet every Pope in the line of succession.

The College of Cardinals

Cardinals are senior clergy, usually bishops, appointed by the Pope as his closest advisers. When a Pope dies or resigns, the cardinals under the age of 80 meet in a conclave in the Sistine Chapel to elect his successor.

The Roman Curia

The offices that help the Pope govern the Church, organized into departments called dicasteries. They handle everything from teaching on doctrine and appointing bishops to promoting the causes of saints.

Ecumenical councils

At key moments in history, the Pope has gathered the world’s bishops in an ecumenical council to address major questions. The most recent was the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965).

Latin and Eastern Catholic Churches

The Catholic Church is a communion of 24 churches. The largest is the Latin (Roman) Church. The other 23 are Eastern Catholic Churches, such as the Ukrainian, Maronite, Melkite and Byzantine Catholic churches. They have their own ancient liturgies and traditions and are fully Catholic, in union with the Pope.

The local Church

Dioceses and bishops

A diocese is a local church covering a defined area, led by a bishop. Bishops are successors of the Apostles. They teach, sanctify and govern the people in their care. In Eastern Catholic Churches a diocese is usually called an eparchy. Large dioceses may also have auxiliary bishops to help the diocesan bishop.

Archdioceses and provinces

Dioceses are grouped into provinces. Each is led by a metropolitan archbishop, who heads the main diocese of the region, the archdiocese. The other dioceses of the province are called suffragan dioceses. See every U.S. diocese.

Bishops’ conferences

The bishops of a country work together through a national conference. In the U.S., this is the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB). The U.S. has about 200 dioceses and eparchies.

Parishes

A parish is a community of the faithful within a diocese, usually serving a neighborhood or town. It is led by a pastor (a priest), sometimes with other priests and deacons. This is where most Catholics go to Mass, are baptized and married, and join groups like OCIA.
