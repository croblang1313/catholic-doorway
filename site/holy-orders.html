Holy Orders and discerning a vocation

Holy Orders is the sacrament by which men are ordained deacons, priests and bishops to serve the Church. If you have ever wondered whether God is calling you to the priesthood, the diaconate or religious life, here is how to start finding out.

What Holy Orders is

Jesus chose twelve apostles and sent them to teach, to celebrate the sacraments and to care for his people. Holy Orders continues that mission. Through the laying on of hands by a bishop, a man is ordained and receives a permanent spiritual mark to serve in the person of Christ. With Marriage, it is one of the two sacraments “at the service of communion,” given for the good of others.

Deacon, priest, bishop

Deacon

Ordained for service. Deacons proclaim the Gospel and preach, baptize, witness marriages, lead funerals and serve the poor. Transitional deacons are on their way to the priesthood; permanent deacons are often married men with jobs and families.

Priest

Celebrates Mass, hears confessions, anoints the sick and shepherds a parish or other community. Diocesan priests serve parishes in one diocese under the bishop; religious priests belong to an order such as the Franciscans, Dominicans or Jesuits.

Bishop

Has the fullness of Holy Orders as a successor of the apostles. A bishop teaches, sanctifies and governs a diocese, ordains deacons and priests, and confirms. Bishops are chosen by the Pope.

See how they fit together on our Church structure page, and what each one wears in What Priests & Deacons Wear.

Am I being called?

  1. Pray

    Ask God plainly what he wants for your life, and listen. Time before the Blessed Sacrament (adoration), regular Mass and Confession make it easier to hear.

  2. Talk to a priest

    Your parish priest has walked this road. Tell him you are wondering. He will not pressure you, and he can help you see the signs.

  3. Contact your diocese’s vocation director

    Every diocese has a priest whose job is to help men discern. Find your diocese in our diocese directory and look on its website for “Vocations.” Many hold discernment retreats and dinners several times a year.

  4. Look at religious communities too

    If community life, a particular mission (teaching, missions, the poor) or a monastery attracts you, visit an order. Most welcome “come and see” weekends.

Signs of a call are often ordinary: a desire that keeps coming back, joy in serving at Mass or helping others, people telling you “you would make a good priest,” and peace when you pray about it. Discernment is a conversation, not a commitment. Seminaries and religious houses are places to find out.

Becoming a priest

  • Who can apply: a Catholic man, usually between about 18 and 45 (some dioceses accept older men), confirmed, single, in good health, and practicing the faith. Men who became Catholic as adults are usually asked to wait a few years after entering the Church.
  • Application: through the vocation director, with interviews, a background check, a psychological evaluation and a medical exam.
  • Seminary: in the U.S., usually a propaedeutic (preparatory) year, then about two years of philosophy and four years of theology, with human and spiritual formation and parish internships along the way. Men who already have a college degree usually need six to eight years in all.
  • Ordination: first as a transitional deacon, then about a year later as a priest.
  • Cost: most dioceses cover much of the cost of seminary. Ask the vocation director about tuition and student loans.

Priests of the Latin (Roman) Church promise celibacy, giving their whole heart to God and his people. Some Eastern Catholic Churches ordain married men, and in special cases the Church ordains married former Protestant and Anglican ministers who become Catholic.

Becoming a permanent deacon

Married or single men, usually between about 35 and 60, can be ordained permanent deacons while keeping their jobs and families. Formation usually takes four to five years of evening and weekend classes, and a married man needs his wife’s consent and support; many programs include wives in the classes. If a deacon’s wife dies, he does not remarry. Ask your pastor or the diocese’s office for the diaconate.

Religious life for women and men

Not every vocation is to Holy Orders. Religious sisters, nuns and brothers make vows of poverty, chastity and obedience and live in community. Some teach, nurse or serve the poor; others live a life of prayer in a monastery. Consecrated virgins and members of secular institutes give their lives to God while living in the world. To explore, visit communities, talk with a sister or brother, and ask your diocese’s vocation office, which helps women as well as men.

An ordination Mass

Ordinations usually take place at the cathedral, and anyone may attend; they are moving to see. After the Gospel the candidates are called by name and answer, “Present.” They promise obedience to the bishop, lie face down while everyone sings the Litany of the Saints, and then the bishop lays his hands on each man’s head in silence and says the prayer of ordination. New priests are vested in stole and chasuble and their hands are anointed with chrism. It is a tradition to ask a newly ordained priest for his first blessing.

Common questions

How do I address a priest or deacon?

Call a priest “Father” (Father Smith, or Father Joe), and a deacon “Deacon” (Deacon Smith). A bishop is “Bishop” or, formally, “Your Excellency.”

Can women be ordained?

The Catholic Church teaches that it has no authority to ordain women as priests, following the example of Christ, who chose men as his apostles. Women serve the Church in many vital ways, including religious life, parish leadership, teaching and chaplaincy.

How can I support vocations?

Pray for priests and seminarians, encourage young people who show signs of a call, and thank your priests and deacons. Many parishes pray a vocations prayer at Mass and host seminarians in the summer.
