Becoming Catholic

Whether you’re just curious or ready to take the next step, the Catholic Church has a welcoming, unhurried path for adults. It’s called OCIA, and it begins with one conversation.

What is OCIA?

OCIA stands for the Order of Christian Initiation of Adults. It used to be called RCIA, and you’ll still hear both names. It is the way adults and older children join the Catholic Church.

OCIA is not a class you pass. It’s a journey you make with others: a small group from a local parish who meet regularly to learn, pray, ask questions and get to know the Catholic way of life. A sponsor, a practicing Catholic from the parish, walks with you along the way.

It’s free, and there’s no pressure. Asking questions doesn’t commit you to anything. Many people spend time in the first stage simply exploring before they decide.

Your first step: contact a parish

OCIA happens at your local parish, so the first step is to reach out to one. You can join any parish you like, though most people choose one near home.

  1. Find a Catholic church near you on our map. Each church has an “Ask about OCIA” button with what to say, the phone number and a ready-made email.
  2. Visit its website, or call or email the parish office.
  3. Ask for the person who leads OCIA. At some parishes this is called RCIA, Adult Faith Formation or Christian Initiation. If you’re not sure, ask for the pastor.

Not sure what to say? Try this

“Hi, my name is ____. I’m interested in learning more about becoming Catholic. Could you put me in touch with whoever leads OCIA? I’d love to know when your group meets.”

If a parish doesn’t answer, try another nearby, or contact your diocese’s office of worship or evangelization. They can point you to a parish with an active group.

The four stages

The Church describes OCIA as four periods, each marked by a special ceremony (a rite) celebrated with the parish. Someone who has never been baptized is called an inquirer at first, then a catechumen, and then one of the elect.

  1. Inquiry

    Formally, the Period of Evangelization and Precatechumenate

    A time to ask questions and hear about Jesus Christ and the Church. There’s no set length and no commitment. When you feel ready to continue, you take the next step.

    Rite of Acceptance into the Order of Catechumens

  2. Catechumenate

    The longest stage. You learn the faith more deeply: the Scriptures, the Creed, the sacraments, prayer and the Christian life. You attend Mass with the parish and often leave after the homily with your group to reflect on the readings together.

    Rite of Election, with the bishop, usually on the First Sunday of Lent

  3. Purification and Enlightenment

    The weeks of Lent, a time of retreat and prayer rather than more classes. The parish prays over you in rites called the Scrutinies on the Third, Fourth and Fifth Sundays of Lent.

    Sacraments of Initiation at the Easter Vigil: Baptism, Confirmation and First Communion

  4. Mystagogy

    From Easter to Pentecost and beyond. As a new Catholic (a neophyte), you reflect on what you’ve received and find your place in the life and service of the parish.

How long does it take?

Most parishes take about 8 to 12 months. A common pattern is to start in late summer or fall and receive the sacraments at the Easter Vigil the following spring. The U.S. bishops’ guidelines ask that someone who has never been baptized spend at least a full year in formation, so your parish may suggest a longer path. The pace is set to fit you, not a calendar.

Already baptized?

If you were baptized in another Christian church (for example Lutheran, Methodist, Baptist, Anglican or Presbyterian), the Catholic Church recognizes your Baptism, and you will not be baptized again. You are called a candidate. You will make a profession of faith and receive Confirmation and First Communion. Your preparation can be shorter and is fitted to what you already know and practice. Bring a copy of your baptismal certificate if you have one.

If you were baptized Catholic but never received First Communion or Confirmation, ask your parish about adult Confirmation preparation.

If you have been married before, mention it early in a private conversation with the priest or OCIA leader. It doesn’t mean you can’t become Catholic, but some situations take extra time to sort out, so it helps to begin early.

For children aged about seven and older who are not baptized, parishes offer a version of OCIA adapted for their age.

What to expect at your first Mass

The Mass is the central act of Catholic worship. It lasts about an hour (a little longer on Sundays) and follows the same shape everywhere in the world:

  1. Introductory Rites: an entrance hymn, the Sign of the Cross, a moment to ask God’s mercy, and on Sundays the hymn “Glory to God.”
  2. Liturgy of the Word: readings from the Bible (usually from the Old Testament, a Psalm, the New Testament and a Gospel), a homily, the Creed and prayers for the world.
  3. Liturgy of the Eucharist: bread and wine are brought to the altar, the priest prays the Eucharistic Prayer, everyone prays the Our Father, and Catholics receive Holy Communion.
  4. Concluding Rites: a blessing and dismissal, usually with a final hymn.

Helpful to know

  • You are welcome. Nobody will single you out. Follow along in the missal or worship aid in the pew, or on the parish’s screen.
  • Standing, sitting and kneeling change throughout. Do what’s comfortable, and simply sit if you prefer.
  • Holy Communion is received by Catholics who are prepared to receive it, because Catholics believe it is truly the Body and Blood of Christ. If you are not Catholic, you’re invited to stay in your seat and pray. In many parishes you may instead come forward with your arms crossed over your chest to receive a blessing.
  • Dress is usually what you’d wear to a nice dinner, but come as you are.
  • Afterward, introduce yourself to the priest at the door and say you’re curious. That’s a perfect way to begin.

Common questions

Questions about Catholic beliefs, like Mary, confession or the Pope? See Honest questions, straight answers.

Does OCIA cost anything?

No. Some parishes may ask for a small amount for books, but no one is turned away.

Do I have to believe everything before I start?

No. OCIA is for people with questions. Bring them. Honest questions are part of the journey.

Can I stop if I change my mind?

Yes. You are free at every stage. The Church asks only that you take each step freely.

Can my spouse or a friend come with me?

Usually, yes. Many groups welcome a Catholic spouse or friend, and they may even become your sponsor.

Where can I read more?

The U.S. bishops have an overview of Christian initiation, and our What Catholics Believe page is a gentle place to start.
